## What the mechanic does

The battery is tested before anything is replaced, because a battery that
will not start the car is not always the faulty part. A load test or a
conductance tester gives a measured figure against the cold cranking amps
printed on the battery label.

If it is replaced, the negative terminal comes off first and goes back on
last, so that a spanner touching bodywork while the positive is still
connected cannot short the battery. The clamp bracket is undone, the old
battery lifted out, and the tray and terminals cleaned of corrosion. The new
battery is secured in its bracket, because a battery left loose cracks its own
case on Sri Lankan road surfaces, and the terminals are greased to slow
corrosion.

## How long it takes

A test takes a few minutes. A replacement is usually twenty to thirty
minutes, longer on cars where the battery sits under a seat or in the boot, or
where the car needs its charging system reconfigured for the new battery.

## What the voltage readings mean

A healthy battery at rest, with the engine off and after the surface charge
has settled, sits around 12.6 volts. Around 12.4 volts is roughly three
quarters charged and below about 12.0 volts is effectively flat. With the
engine running the reading should climb to somewhere around 13.8 to 14.4
volts, which is the alternator charging. A running engine that does not lift
the voltage above resting level points to a charging fault rather than a dead
battery.

## Cranking is the real test

Voltage dips sharply while the starter turns. A brief dip is normal, but a
battery that collapses far down during cranking and recovers afterwards is
failing under load even if it reads acceptably at rest. This is why a resting
voltage alone never confirms a good battery.

## The battery may not be the fault

A battery that goes flat repeatedly after being replaced usually means
something else. A failing alternator will not replenish what starting consumes.
A parasitic drain, often an accessory or a badly fitted alarm, empties the
battery while the car is parked. Corroded or loose terminals produce
intermittent starting that looks exactly like a dying battery. A garage that
replaces the battery without asking why the last one died may well see you
again.

## Heat is what kills batteries here

Battery life is shortened far more by heat than by cold. Sustained high
under-bonnet temperatures accelerate the internal chemistry and evaporate
electrolyte, which is why batteries in tropical climates often reach the end
of their life sooner than the manufacturer's stated figure suggests. Short
journeys make it worse, because starting takes a large amount of charge that a
brief drive never fully replaces.

## What to check afterwards

Check the new battery is clamped down and cannot be rocked by hand. Confirm
the rating on the label matches or exceeds what the car calls for. Ask for the
test reading if you were told the old one had failed, and note the date of
fitting, since the warranty runs from it.
