## Squealing when you brake

Most pads carry a small metal tab called a wear indicator. When the friction
material wears down to a few millimetres the tab touches the disc and makes a
high squeal that stops when you release the pedal. This is the pad telling you
it is near the end of its life. It is a booking, not an emergency, but it
should not be left for months.

## Grinding or a harsh scraping noise

Grinding usually means the friction material is gone and metal is running on
metal. Braking distance is longer, and the disc is being cut into every time
you stop. This changes the job from a pad replacement into a pad and disc
replacement, which costs considerably more. Stop driving on it.

## A soft or sinking pedal

If the pedal feels spongy, or slowly sinks toward the floor when held under
steady pressure, the problem is usually in the hydraulic system rather than
the pads. Air in the lines, a fluid leak, or a failing master cylinder all
present this way. This is not a wear item and it does not wait.

## Vibration or pulsing through the pedal

A pulsing you can feel through the pedal under braking normally points to
discs that are no longer running true, often from heat. It is felt most
clearly when slowing from higher speed. Pads alone will not fix it.

## The car pulls to one side

Pulling under braking suggests one side is doing more work than the other. A
sticking caliper slide pin, a seized piston, or unevenly worn pads all cause
it. Left alone, the hard-working side overheats and wears much faster.

## Why heavy traffic wears pads faster

Pad life depends far more on how many times you brake than on how far you
drive. Stop-start commuting in Colombo traffic can wear a set of pads in
noticeably less distance than the same kilometres on open road, because every
slow-down is another cycle of friction and heat. A pad life figure quoted as a
distance always assumes some average driving pattern, so treat it as a guide
rather than a promise.
