## What the mechanic does

The engine is warmed briefly so the oil flows and carries suspended dirt with
it. The car is raised, the sump plug is removed and the old oil is drained
into a tray. While it drains, the oil filter is unscrewed and replaced, and
the new filter's rubber seal is smeared with clean oil so it seats without
tearing.

The sump plug goes back with a new crush washer where the manufacturer
specifies one, tightened to a set torque rather than by feel. Fresh oil is
poured in to the correct quantity for that engine, the level is checked on the
dipstick after the oil has had a minute to settle, and the engine is run
briefly to confirm the oil pressure light goes out and nothing weeps from the
filter or the plug.

## How long it takes

An oil and filter change is typically thirty to forty-five minutes. It is
often bundled with a general inspection, which is what turns it into a
half-day booking at a busy garage.

## Why the interval matters more than the distance

Oil degrades with heat cycles and time as well as with kilometres. An engine
that idles for long periods in traffic accumulates far more running hours per
kilometre than one on open road, so a car used mainly in Colombo traffic is
working its oil harder than the odometer suggests. Where a manufacturer gives
both a distance and a time limit, whichever comes first is the one that
applies.

## What using the wrong grade does

Oil grade is not a preference. Too thick and the oil is slow to reach the top
of the engine on a cold start, which is when most wear happens. Too thin and
the film between moving parts breaks down under load and heat. The correct
grade is in the owner's handbook and usually printed on the oil filler cap.

## Signs the oil is overdue

Very dark, thick oil on the dipstick, a ticking or tapping from the top of the
engine on start-up that fades after a few seconds, or an oil level that has
fallen noticeably between checks. A car burning or leaking oil needs the cause
found, not just a top-up.

## What to check afterwards

Ask which grade and how many litres went in, and check that against the
handbook. Look at the dipstick yourself once the car has stood for a few
minutes. Check the ground where the car was parked overnight for fresh drips
near the front. A service sticker or a stamped book entry with the date and
odometer reading is what makes the next interval calculable.
