## Spark plugs

Plugs wear at the electrode, and the gap widens as they age. A worn plug
demands more voltage to fire, which shows up as rough idle, hesitation under
acceleration, and worse fuel consumption long before it becomes an outright
misfire. Replacement is straightforward on most engines and the plugs are
always changed as a full set.

## Air filter

A clogged air filter restricts the air the engine can draw, and the engine
management compensates by trimming the fuel mixture. It is a cheap part and a
quick change, and it is one of the first things to check when fuel consumption
has crept up without another explanation. Dusty conditions and heavy traffic
shorten its life considerably.

## Timing belt or chain

A timing belt is a scheduled replacement item with an interval in both
kilometres and years. On many engines a snapped belt lets the valves and
pistons meet, which turns a planned service into an engine rebuild. This is the
single most expensive thing to defer on a car that uses a belt. Chains
generally last longer but stretch, and a rattle for a second or two on start-up
is the usual first sign.

## Coolant

Coolant does more than stop the engine boiling. It carries corrosion
inhibitors that are consumed over time, and once they are gone the radiator,
the water pump and the internal passages begin to corrode even though the
coolant still looks brightly coloured. It is replaced on an interval, not on
appearance.

## Fuel trim readings and what they suggest

Long term fuel trim shows how far the engine management has had to move away
from its baseline fuel calculation to keep the mixture correct. Small values
either side of zero are normal. A consistently large positive trim means the
system is adding fuel, which points toward unmetered air entering the engine,
a weak fuel supply, or a tiring oxygen sensor. A large negative trim means it
is taking fuel away. Either reading is a symptom that narrows the search
rather than a fault in itself.

## Why an overheating engine is never a wait-and-see

Aluminium cylinder heads distort quickly once coolant temperature runs away,
and a warped head or a failed head gasket costs many times what the original
cause would have. If the temperature gauge climbs past its normal position,
the correct response is to stop and let the engine cool rather than press on
to the next town.
