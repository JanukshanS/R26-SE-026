## What the mechanic does

The wheel is removed and taken to a tyre machine. The bead of the old tyre is
broken away from the rim, the tyre is levered off, and the rim is checked for
buckles and for corrosion on the sealing face. The new tyre is fitted with
lubricant on the bead, inflated until the beads seat with an audible pop, then
set to the correct pressure.

The wheel then goes on a balancing machine, which spins it to find where the
assembly is heavy and calls for small weights to be clipped or stuck to the rim
to counter it. Balancing is not optional: an unbalanced wheel produces a
vibration that appears at a particular speed and wears both the tyre and the
suspension.

## How long it takes

Fitting and balancing takes roughly twenty to thirty minutes per wheel. Wheel
alignment, if it is being done, is a separate operation on a different machine
and adds most of an hour.

## Balancing and alignment are different things

Balancing corrects weight distribution around the wheel and cures vibration.
Alignment corrects the angles at which the wheels point and cures pulling and
uneven wear across the tread. A vibration that appears at a certain speed is a
balance problem. A car that drifts to one side, or wears one shoulder of the
tyre, is an alignment problem. Paying for one will not fix the other.

## Tread depth and why it matters most in rain

Tread exists to give water somewhere to go. As depth falls, the tyre's ability
to clear water drops sharply, and the speed at which it will aquaplane falls
with it. In monsoon conditions a tyre near the legal limit behaves very
differently from a new one, even though both look perfectly serviceable when
parked. Tread wear indicators are moulded into the grooves so the limit can be
seen without a gauge.

## Reading uneven wear

Wear on both outer shoulders usually means chronic under-inflation. Wear
concentrated in the centre band means over-inflation. Wear on one shoulder
only points to alignment. A scalloped or feathered pattern you can feel by
running a hand across the tread usually means worn suspension components
rather than a problem with the tyre itself.

## Age matters as well as tread

Rubber hardens with time and heat, and a tyre with plenty of tread can still
be too old to grip well. The manufacturing date is moulded into the sidewall
as a four digit code: week, then year. Sri Lanka's sustained heat and strong
sunlight age sidewalls faster than a temperate climate does, so it is worth
reading that date on tyres being sold as new.

## What to check afterwards

Confirm all four pressures with your own gauge once the tyres are cold, since
a tyre inflated while hot will read low later. Check that balance weights are
actually present on the new wheels. Ask for the alignment printout if alignment
was charged for. On the first drive at highway speed, note any vibration
through the steering wheel or the seat.
